Why Respire works the way it does

Updated: November 4, 2026

The app keeps only what you need. The explanations are here, rule by rule: what it does, why it is there and what research says about it. When a rule rests on a design choice rather than a study, this page says so.

Study: a published result supports the rule.

Design choice: no study tests this rule directly. It follows from a related finding or from common sense, and it may change if your feedback shows it gets in the way.

Principles

  • You stay in control. Closing the app is always possible right away, and every rule can be adjusted or turned off. In the study of one sec, the app Respire builds on, the option to give up in one tap had the strongest effect. In online health interventions, offering a real choice also mattered more than the tone of the messages.
  • Everything is explained. Saying that a mechanism is there to steer you does not make it less effective. That is why this page exists.
  • Helping you understand rather than steering you. The statistics are there so you can see for yourself when the urge comes back, and tune Respire your own way.
  • Nothing to make you feel guilty. No score, no streak, no badge, no notification. A day with many opens is information, not a failure.

The pause before opening

When you open a chosen app, a breathing screen appears with a countdown (10 seconds by default). The close button is available right away; the open button only when the countdown ends.

The aim is to break the automatic gesture: a few seconds are enough to ask yourself whether you really want to go in.

Study: among 280 people followed for six weeks, one sec cut actual openings of the targeted apps by 57%. The option to give up had the strongest effect, the delay also helped, and the reflection message alone had no measurable effect (Grüning et al., 2023). That is why the question on screen stays short and the Close button never makes you wait.

The growing wait

Each new attempt within the same hour adds 5 seconds by default. The wait is doubled during risky hours and never exceeds 90 seconds.

Coming back several times within an hour is often a reflex rather than a need. The pause gets firmer as the urge repeats.

Design choice: the effect of a delay is established, but no published study compares a fixed wait with a growing one. The 90-second cap keeps a useful opening from becoming a chore.

Session end

Opening an app gives you 10 minutes by default. If you are still there, the pause comes back in the middle of the feed.

An endless feed offers no natural moment to stop. Session end creates one.

Design choice: the studies cited here look at the moment of opening and at daily limits, not at a pause during use.

The daily limit

After a number of opens per app (6 by default), the app stays closed until tomorrow. Extending a session counts as an open, so the limit cannot be bypassed through session end.

Study: in a controlled trial, being able to set daily limits that are hard to override reduced social media use by about 16% (Allcott, Gentzkow and Song, 2022).

Risky hours

Off by default. You choose a time range (10 pm to 7 am unless you change it). During that range, the wait is doubled and opens are capped, at zero by default. Respire suggests a range based on your own hourly data.

The urge is not the same at every hour. Rather than a general rule, Respire starts from what your own numbers show.

Design choice: tuning the tool from your own data follows the so-called boost approach, which helps people decide for themselves rather than steering them (Hertwig and Grüne-Yanoff, 2017). The effect of this range has not been measured.

The Reels and Shorts filter

Off by default. When on, Respire leaves Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts as soon as they open. Messages, search and regular videos stay available. The Facebook filter is in testing and does not block anything yet.

Short videos follow one another endlessly. The filter removes that format without cutting off the rest of the app, which you may need.

Design choice: no study measures the effect of such a filter.

The lock on loosening

Removing an app or relaxing a rule takes a 30-second wait. Nothing is forbidden: after the wait, the change applies. And you can always turn Respire off in Accessibility settings.

Study: looking at real-world use by more than 1,000 one sec users, researchers saw frequent breaks from the tool, during which use goes back up, then drops again quickly once the tool is back on (Haliburton et al., 2024). The lock targets loosening on a whim, not considered changes.

Your week

Pauses, opens and give-ups by day, by hour and by app, over the last 7 days.

These numbers show when temptation is strongest so you can tune Respire accordingly. They give no grade and no goal.

Design choice: same principle as risky hours.

What Respire does not do

  • It does not measure your screen time or read the content of your apps.
  • It sends no notifications and shows no tip of the day.
  • It blocks nothing for good: every rule can be changed or removed.

Limits

Respire has not been evaluated in a study. The results cited here concern one sec and a comparable limit tool, not Respire itself. If a rule does not help you or gets in your way, tell us or write to pilmil.studio@proton.me.
